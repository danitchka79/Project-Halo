About

Across the Pacific Islands, ports, seawalls and reclamation have replaced many of the mangroves that once buffered coastlines from waves and storms. Project Halophyte, a collaboration between UNSW Sydney's Water Research Laboratory and the University of the South Pacific funded by Swire Shipping, is developing ways to bring them back, through tidal restoration, Mangrove-Integrated Coastal Infrastructure (MICI) and research on what these solutions mean for coastal communities.

MICI plants mangroves on modular floating pontoons, so they can grow along built shorelines, in marinas and in harbours where land-based planting is no longer possible.

MICI is designed to be multifunctional rather than a single-purpose planter. The same structure can shelter the shore, provide habitat for fish, birds and invertebrates, and store carbon, while also working as a walkway, kayak or boat landing, fishing deck, mangrove nursery or base for aquaculture. Combining these uses puts more value into one structure, spreads the cost across more of the people who benefit, and gives communities, resorts and marina operators a reason to build and look after it.

The project is testing MICI in Sydney and Suva, Fiji, and assessing candidate sites across Fiji, the Solomon Islands and the wider Pacific.

Tools

Module design guide

Step-by-step questions from configuration to mooring, with trade-offs for each option and a built-in float calculator.

Float comparison calculator

Compares float layouts for the same planter: planting ratio, plastic use, freeboard, stability and natural periods.

Lagoon builder

A workshop game: lay out pontoons, mangroves and amenities on a lagoon and see the trade-offs.

Marina design checks

Live calculators for pontoon stability, layout, waves, loads, mooring and access, comparing AS 3962 and the SRSA marina code.
